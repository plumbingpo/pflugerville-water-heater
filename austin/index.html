Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Austin

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling every year in hard-water areas.

The age of your heater matters just as much. With regular maintenance, a standard tank usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts acting up, like discolored hot water, rumbling, or a damp spot around the base, it's smart to start planning a replacement. A planned replacement is cheaper and causes far less stress than an emergency swap with water spreading across the floor.

Small problems rarely stay small when they're ignored. Take a spent anode rod: replacing it is a short, inexpensive repair, but once it's gone, the water begins corroding the tank steel, and just like that a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to keep our number in their phone and call at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and prevents a surprise cleanup.

Hot Water Headaches Austin Homeowners Face

Around Austin, water heater trouble usually come from a handful of causes. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be a real factor: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

We also see a lot of aging equipment. A standard tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, and by then the anode rod is often eaten away, leaving the steel open to rust. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Every house is set up a little differently. A home on a private well may see more sediment and sometimes a sulfur smell in the hot water, often fixed by switching the anode rod type. A tankless unit on hard water needs regular descaling. And an older installation being replaced often needs code items that weren't required when it went in: an expansion tank, a properly routed relief valve line, a drain pan, or strapping where required. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most are much cheaper when caught early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
